Unit 5: Financial Decision Making and Cost of Capital

Introduction

One of the most important responsibilities of a manager is making financial decisions that maximize the value of the organization. Businesses regularly invest in projects such as purchasing machinery, expanding production facilities, launching new products, or entering new markets. These decisions require careful evaluation because they involve large amounts of money and affect future profitability.

At the same time, businesses need funds to finance these investments. The cost incurred in obtaining funds from different sources is known as the Cost of Capital. Understanding investment evaluation techniques and cost of capital helps managers make sound financial decisions.

Part A: Financial Decision Making

Topic 1: Nature and Features of Capital Budgeting Decisions

Explanation

Capital Budgeting refers to the process of evaluating and selecting long-term investment projects.

Examples include:

  • Purchase of machinery
  • Construction of a factory
  • Launch of a new product line
  • Expansion into new markets

Features of Capital Budgeting Decisions

Feature Explanation
Long-Term Impact Benefits extend over many years
Large Investment Requires significant funds
Irreversible Nature Difficult to reverse once implemented
Future-Oriented Based on expected future cash flows
Risk and Uncertainty Future outcomes may vary

Importance

  • Maximizes shareholder wealth.
  • Ensures efficient use of resources.
  • Supports business growth.
  • Improves profitability.

Example 1 (Business): Tesla invests billions in new manufacturing plants after evaluating long-term benefits.

Example 2 (Practical): A restaurant owner decides whether to purchase a new automated kitchen system to increase efficiency.

Topic 2: Types of Investment Decisions

Explanation

Investment decisions can be classified into different categories.

1. Expansion Decisions

Investments made to increase production capacity.
Example: Building a new factory.

2. Replacement Decisions

Replacing old equipment with new technology.
Example: Replacing outdated machinery.

3. Diversification Decisions

Entering new product lines or markets.
Example: A mobile phone company launching laptops.

4. Modernization Decisions

Upgrading existing facilities.
Example: Installing advanced software systems.

Example 1 (Business): Amazon continuously invests in new warehouses and logistics infrastructure for expansion.

Example 2 (Practical): A printing shop replaces an old printer with a faster digital printer.

Topic 4: Net Present Value (NPV)

Explanation

NPV is one of the most reliable capital budgeting techniques. It compares the present value of future cash inflows with the initial investment.

Formula

$$\text{NPV} = \text{Present Value of Cash Inflows} - \text{Initial Investment}$$

Decision Rule

NPV Decision
Positive Accept Project
Zero Indifferent
Negative Reject Project

Advantages

  • Considers time value of money.
  • Considers all cash flows.
  • Maximizes shareholder wealth.

Limitations

  • Requires estimation of discount rate.
  • More complex than traditional methods.

Example 1 (Business): A company invests ₹10 lakh and expects discounted future cash inflows worth ₹13 lakh.
$$\text{NPV} = \text{₹}13\text{ lakh} - \text{₹}10\text{ lakh} = \text{₹}3\text{ lakh}$$
Project should be accepted.

Example 2 (Practical): A shop owner invests in a vending machine after calculating a positive NPV.

Topic 5: Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

Explanation

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV becomes zero.

Concept

At IRR:

$$\text{NPV} = 0$$

Decision Rule

Condition Decision
IRR > Cost of Capital Accept
IRR < Cost of Capital Reject

Advantages

  • Easy interpretation.
  • Considers time value of money.
  • Widely used in practice.

Limitations

  • Complex calculations.
  • Multiple IRRs may occur in some projects.

Example 1 (Business): A company accepts a project with an IRR of 18% when its cost of capital is 12%.

Example 2 (Practical): An entrepreneur invests in a franchise only if the expected return exceeds borrowing costs.

Topic 6: Profitability Index (PI)

Explanation

Profitability Index measures the value created per unit of investment.

Formula

$$\text{PI} = \frac{\text{Present Value of Cash Inflows}}{\text{Initial Investment}}$$

Decision Rule

PI Decision
Greater than 1 Accept
Equal to 1 Indifferent
Less than 1 Reject

Advantages

  • Useful when capital is limited.
  • Considers time value of money.

Example 1 (Business): A project requiring ₹5 lakh investment generates present value cash inflows of ₹6 lakh.
$$\text{PI} = 6,00,000 \div 5,00,000 = 1.2$$
Accept the project.

Example 2 (Practical): A retailer compares multiple investment opportunities using PI.

Topic 7: Payback Period

Explanation

Payback Period measures the time required to recover the initial investment.

Formula

$$\text{Payback Period} = \frac{\text{Initial Investment}}{\text{Annual Cash Inflow}}$$

Decision Rule

Projects with shorter payback periods are generally preferred.

Advantages

  • Simple and easy.
  • Focuses on liquidity.

Limitations

  • Ignores time value of money.
  • Ignores cash flows after payback.

Example 1 (Business): Investment = ₹4,00,000
Annual Cash Inflow = ₹1,00,000
$$\text{Payback Period} = 4\text{ Years}$$

Example 2 (Practical): A person purchases a solar system and calculates how many years are needed to recover the investment.

Topic 8: Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

Explanation

ARR measures profitability based on accounting profits.

Formula

$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average Annual Profit}}{\text{Average Investment}} \times 100$$

Decision Rule

Higher ARR indicates a more attractive project.

Advantages

  • Simple calculation.
  • Uses accounting information.

Limitations

  • Ignores time value of money.
  • Based on accounting profit rather than cash flow.

Example 1 (Business): A project with average annual profit of ₹50,000 and average investment of ₹2,50,000:
$$\text{ARR} = 20\%$$

Example 2 (Practical): A business owner compares two projects using ARR before selecting one.

Comparison of Capital Budgeting Techniques

Method Time Value of Money Ease of Use Decision Basis

IRR Yes Moderate Return Percentage

Part B: Cost of Capital

Topic 9: Concept and Importance of Cost of Capital

Explanation

Cost of Capital is the minimum rate of return that a company must earn on its investments to satisfy investors and creditors.

Importance

  • Capital budgeting decisions
  • Financing decisions
  • Business valuation
  • Performance evaluation

Sources of Capital

  • Debt
  • Preference Shares
  • Equity Shares
  • Retained Earnings

Example 1 (Business): A company compares the cost of borrowing with expected project returns before investing.

Example 2 (Practical): An entrepreneur evaluates whether expected profits justify the interest payable on a business loan.

Topic 10: Cost of Debt

Explanation

Cost of Debt refers to the effective cost of borrowed funds.

Formula

$$\text{Cost of Debt} = \frac{\text{Annual Interest}}{\text{Net Proceeds}} \times 100$$

Features

  • Usually the cheapest source of finance.
  • Interest is tax deductible.

Example 1 (Business): A company borrows ₹10 lakh at 10% interest. Annual interest = ₹1 lakh.
$$\text{Cost of Debt} = 10\%$$

Example 2 (Practical): A shop owner takes a bank loan and calculates its borrowing cost.

Topic 11: Cost of Preference Capital

Explanation

Preference shareholders receive fixed dividends.

Formula

$$\text{Cost of Preference Capital} = \frac{\text{Annual Preference Dividend}}{\text{Net Proceeds}} \times 100$$

Example 1 (Business): A company issues preference shares paying fixed annual dividends.

Example 2 (Practical): An investor purchases preference shares expecting regular income.

Topic 12: Cost of Equity Capital

Explanation

Cost of Equity represents the return expected by equity shareholders.

Formula

$$\text{Cost of Equity} = \frac{\text{Dividend}}{\text{Market Price}} + \text{Growth Rate}$$

Importance

  • Most important component of overall cost of capital.
  • Reflects shareholder expectations.

Example 1 (Business): A company evaluates expected shareholder returns before issuing new shares.

Example 2 (Practical): An investor expects dividend income plus future growth in share value.

Topic 13: Cost of Retained Earnings

Explanation

Retained earnings represent profits reinvested in the business instead of being distributed as dividends.

Features

  • Internal source of finance.
  • Has an opportunity cost.
  • Shareholders expect returns from retained profits.

Example 1 (Business): Microsoft retains part of its profits to finance future growth.

Example 2 (Practical): A business owner reinvests annual profits instead of withdrawing them.

Topic 14: Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

Explanation

WACC represents the average cost of all sources of finance used by a company.

Formula

$$\text{WACC} = \sum (\text{Weight of Capital} \times \text{Cost of Capital})$$

Importance

  • Used as a discount rate in NPV calculations.
  • Measures overall financing cost.
  • Supports investment decisions.

Advantages

  • Considers all funding sources.
  • Provides a comprehensive cost measure.

Example 1 (Business): A company calculates WACC before evaluating a large investment project.

Example 2 (Practical): An entrepreneur compares project returns with overall financing costs.

Quick Revision Summary

  • Capital budgeting helps evaluate long-term investments.
  • NPV, IRR, PI, Payback Period, and ARR are major investment evaluation techniques.
  • NPV and IRR consider the time value of money.
  • Payback Period focuses on recovery of investment.
  • Cost of Capital is the minimum required return.
  • Major sources of capital include debt, preference shares, equity, and retained earnings.
  • WACC represents the overall cost of financing.
