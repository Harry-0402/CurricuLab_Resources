Unit 1: Introduction to Accounting and Transaction Processing

Introduction

Accounting is often called the language of business because it records, summarizes, and communicates financial information about an organization. Every business, whether small or large, needs accounting to monitor its financial position, evaluate performance, and make informed decisions.

This unit introduces the basic concepts, principles, and procedures of accounting. It explains how business transactions are recorded through journals and ledgers using the double-entry system and highlights the role of accounting standards in ensuring uniformity and transparency in financial reporting.

Topic 1: Accounting Concepts, Conventions, and Principles

Explanation

Accounting concepts, conventions, and principles provide the foundation for recording and reporting financial transactions. They ensure consistency, reliability, and comparability of financial statements.

Accounting Concepts

1. Business Entity Concept

The business is treated as a separate entity from its owner.

Example: If the owner invests ₹1,00,000 in the business, it is recorded as capital and not as business income.

2. Going Concern Concept

A business is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future.

Example: Machinery is valued at cost less depreciation because the business is expected to continue using it.

3. Money Measurement Concept

Only transactions measurable in monetary terms are recorded.

Example: Employee loyalty is important but cannot be recorded because it cannot be measured in money.

4. Accounting Period Concept

Business activities are divided into specific periods for reporting purposes.

Example: Companies prepare annual financial statements for each financial year.

5. Cost Concept

Assets are recorded at their original purchase cost.

Example: Land purchased for ₹5,00,000 is recorded at that amount even if its market value increases.

Accounting Conventions

Convention of Conservatism

Anticipate losses but not profits.

Convention of Consistency

Use the same accounting methods year after year.

Convention of Full Disclosure

Provide all significant information in financial statements.

Convention of Materiality

Record and disclose information that significantly affects decisions.

Accounting Principles

Revenue Recognition Principle

Revenue is recognized when earned.

Matching Principle

Expenses are matched with the revenues they help generate.

Objectivity Principle

Transactions should be supported by evidence.

Example 1 (Business): Tata Motors records vehicle sales revenue when vehicles are delivered to customers, following the revenue recognition principle.

Example 2 (Practical): A grocery shop records electricity expenses for the month in which sales were made, following the matching principle.

Topic 2: Accounting Equation

Explanation

The accounting equation is the foundation of the accounting system.

Formula

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Capital}$$

The equation shows that all resources owned by a business are financed either through liabilities or owner's capital.

Components

Component Meaning
Assets Resources owned by business
Liabilities Amounts owed to outsiders
Capital Owner's claim on assets

Illustration

Owner starts business with ₹1,00,000.

Assets Amount
Cash ₹1,00,000
Liabilities ₹0
Capital ₹1,00,000

Equation:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Capital}$$

$$\text{₹}1,00,000 = \text{₹}0 + \text{₹}1,00,000$$

Effects of Transactions

Transaction Effect
Owner invests cash Assets ↑ Capital ↑
Purchase on credit Assets ↑ Liabilities ↑
Repayment of loan Assets ↓ Liabilities ↓
Profit earned Capital ↑
Loss incurred Capital ↓

Example 1 (Business): Infosys purchases computers worth ₹10 lakh through a bank loan. Assets and liabilities both increase by ₹10 lakh.

Example 2 (Practical): A person starts a tuition center with ₹50,000 cash. Cash increases and owner's capital also increases by ₹50,000.

Topic 3: International Accounting Principles and Standards

Explanation

Accounting standards are guidelines that ensure financial statements are prepared uniformly and transparently.

Objectives

  • Improve comparability
  • Increase reliability
  • Enhance transparency
  • Facilitate international business

Major International Standards

IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards)

Issued by the: International Accounting Standards Board

These standards are accepted in many countries worldwide.

Benefits of IFRS

  • Global comparability
  • Better investor confidence
  • Easier international financing
  • Improved transparency

Example 1 (Business): Unilever uses globally accepted accounting standards to present financial reports across different countries.

Example 2 (Practical): An investor comparing companies from different countries can understand financial statements more easily when common standards are followed.

Topic 4: Matching of Indian Accounting Standards with International Accounting Standards

Explanation

India has adopted accounting standards known as Ind AS (Indian Accounting Standards), which are largely aligned with IFRS.

Purpose

  • Reduce differences between Indian and global reporting.
  • Improve credibility of Indian companies.
  • Facilitate foreign investment.

Key Benefits

Benefit Explanation
Global Comparability Easier comparison with foreign companies
Better Transparency Improves quality of reporting
Investor Confidence Attracts domestic and foreign investors
International Recognition Supports global expansion

Ind AS vs IFRS

Basis Ind AS IFRS
Applicability India International
Issued By Ministry of Corporate Affairs (India) IASB
Objective Indian compliance with global standards Global reporting framework

Example 1 (Business): Reliance Industries prepares financial statements under Ind AS, making them comparable with international firms.

Example 2 (Practical): A foreign investor evaluating an Indian company can better understand its financial statements due to Ind AS alignment with IFRS.

Topic 5: Double Entry System of Accounting

Explanation

The double-entry system is the most widely used accounting system.

Principle

  • Every transaction affects at least two accounts.
  • For every debit, there must be an equal credit.

Fundamental Rule

$$\text{Total Debits} = \text{Total Credits}$$

Advantages

  • Accurate recording
  • Error detection
  • Complete transaction records
  • Support for the preparation of financial statements

Golden Rules

Account Type Debit Credit
Personal Account Receiver Giver
Real Account What comes in What goes out
Nominal Account Expenses & Losses Income & Gains

Example 1 (Business): A company purchases machinery worth ₹50,000 in cash.

Machinery Account Dr  ₹50,000
    Cash Account Cr      ₹50,000

Example 2 (Practical): A shopkeeper receives ₹10,000 from a customer.

Cash Account Dr      ₹10,000
    Customer Account Cr  ₹10,000

Topic 6: Journal

Explanation

A journal is the book of original entry where transactions are recorded chronologically.

Format of Journal

Date Particulars Debit (₹) Credit (₹)
XX Account Dr. xxx
    To Account xxx

Steps in Journalizing

Transaction Occurs
       ↓
Identify Accounts
       ↓
Apply Accounting Rules
       ↓
Determine Debit & Credit
       ↓
Record Journal Entry

Example

Started business with cash ₹1,00,000.

Particulars Debit (₹) Credit (₹)
Cash A/c Dr. 1,00,000
    To Capital A/c 1,00,000

Example 1 (Business): A manufacturing company records all daily transactions first in the journal before posting them to ledger accounts.

Example 2 (Practical): A small retailer records daily cash sales in the journal book.

Topic 7: Ledger Posting of Transactions

Explanation

A ledger is the principal book of accounts where journal entries are classified and grouped account-wise.

Purpose

  • Determine account balances.
  • Facilitate trial balance preparation.
  • Support financial statement preparation.

Posting Process

Transaction
     ↓
Journal Entry
     ↓
Ledger Posting
     ↓
Account Balancing
     ↓
Trial Balance

Sample Ledger

Cash Account

Dr (Debit) Cr (Credit)
Particulars Amount (₹) Particulars Amount (₹)
To Capital A/c 1,00,000 By Purchases A/c 20,000
By Balance c/d 80,000
Total 1,00,000 Total 1,00,000

Balance = ₹80,000

Advantages of Ledger

  • Organized records
  • Easy balance determination
  • Supports financial reporting
  • Assists in error detection

Example 1 (Business): A company posts all sales transactions from the journal into the Sales Ledger to monitor customer balances.

Example 2 (Practical): A tuition center posts fee receipts into the Cash Ledger to determine monthly collections.
