Unit 4: Cash Flow Statement and Inventory Valuation

Introduction

Profit alone does not indicate the financial health of a business. A company may show profits but still face cash shortages. Therefore, managers need to understand how cash moves into and out of the business. The Cash Flow Statement helps in analyzing cash movements, while Inventory Valuation helps determine the value of stock and its impact on profits.

This unit covers the preparation and interpretation of Cash Flow Statements (Indirect Method) and various Inventory Valuation methods such as FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted Average Cost.

Part A: Cash Flow Statement

Topic 1: Introduction to Cash Flow Statement

Explanation

A Cash Flow Statement is a financial statement that shows the inflow and outflow of cash and cash equivalents during a specific accounting period. It explains how cash is generated and used by a business.

Definition

According to accounting standards, a Cash Flow Statement summarizes cash receipts and cash payments during a period.

Importance

  • Measures liquidity position.
  • Helps in financial planning.
  • Assesses ability to pay debts.
  • Assists investors and creditors.
  • Evaluates cash management efficiency.

Example 1 (Business): Apple Inc. generates huge profits, but investors also examine its cash flow statement to assess actual cash generation.

Example 2 (Practical): A shopkeeper may earn profits on credit sales but still lack cash to pay suppliers. Cash flow analysis helps identify such situations.

Topic 2: Purpose and Structure of Cash Flow Statement

Explanation

The main purpose of the Cash Flow Statement is to explain changes in cash during a period.

Objectives

  • Determine sources of cash.
  • Determine uses of cash.
  • Assess liquidity.
  • Evaluate financial flexibility.
  • Support decision-making.

Structure of Cash Flow Statement

Cash Flow Statement
↓
Operating Activities
↓
Investing Activities
↓
Financing Activities

A. Operating Activities

Activities related to normal business operations.

Examples:

  • Cash received from customers
  • Cash paid to suppliers
  • Salary payments
  • Rent payments

B. Investing Activities

Activities involving acquisition and disposal of long-term assets.

Examples:

  • Purchase of machinery
  • Sale of equipment
  • Purchase of investments
  • Sale of investments

C. Financing Activities

Activities involving capital and borrowing.

Examples:

  • Issue of shares
  • Taking loans
  • Repayment of loans
  • Payment of dividends

Example 1 (Business): A manufacturing company purchases machinery worth ₹10 lakh. This is recorded under investing activities.

Example 2 (Practical): An entrepreneur obtains a bank loan to start a business. This appears under financing activities.

Topic 3: Indirect Method of Cash Flow Statement

Explanation

The syllabus specifies preparation of Cash Flow Statement using the Indirect Method only. Under this method, cash flow from operating activities starts with net profit and adjusts for non-cash and non-operating items.

Steps

Net Profit Before Tax
↓
Add Non-Cash Expenses
↓
Adjust Non-Operating Items
↓
Adjust Changes in Working Capital
↓
Net Cash from Operating Activities

Adjustments Required

Add Back Non-Cash Expenses

  • Depreciation
  • Amortization
  • Goodwill written off

Deduct Non-Operating Incomes

  • Profit on sale of assets
  • Dividend income
  • Interest received

Working Capital Adjustments

Increase In Effect Decrease In Effect
Current Assets Deduct Current Assets Add
Current Liabilities Add Current Liabilities Deduct

Example 1 (Business): A company adds depreciation of ₹50,000 back to net profit because depreciation does not involve cash outflow.

Example 2 (Practical): A business records profit on credit sales, but actual cash has not yet been received. Working capital adjustments account for this difference.

Topic 4: Computing Net Cash Flow from Operating Activities

Explanation

Operating activities represent the primary revenue-generating activities of a business.

Formula

$$\text{Net Profit Before Tax}$$

$$+ \text{ Non-Cash Expenses}$$

$$\pm \text{ Working Capital Changes}$$

$$= \text{ Net Cash Flow from Operating Activities}$$

Illustration

Particulars Amount (₹)
Net Profit 1,00,000
Add: Depreciation 20,000
Less: Increase in Debtors (10,000)
Add: Increase in Creditors 5,000
Net Operating Cash Flow 1,15,000

Example 1 (Business): A company with high depreciation expenses may report strong operating cash flow despite moderate accounting profits.

Example 2 (Practical): A retailer receives advance payments from customers, increasing operating cash flow.

Topic 5: Computing Cash Flow from Investing Activities

Explanation

Investing activities involve long-term assets and investments.

Cash Inflows

  • Sale of machinery
  • Sale of investments
  • Interest received
  • Dividend received

Cash Outflows

  • Purchase of machinery
  • Purchase of buildings
  • Purchase of investments

Illustration

Particulars Amount (₹)
Sale of Equipment +50,000
Purchase of Machinery -1,20,000
Net Investing Cash Flow -70,000

Negative cash flow often indicates expansion and growth.

Example 1 (Business): Tata Steel purchases new production equipment, resulting in investing cash outflow.

Example 2 (Practical): A shop owner purchases a delivery vehicle, creating an investing activity outflow.

Topic 6: Computing Cash Flow from Financing Activities

Explanation

Financing activities relate to sources and repayment of long-term funds.

Cash Inflows

  • Issue of shares
  • Issue of debentures
  • Bank loans

Cash Outflows

  • Loan repayment
  • Redemption of debentures
  • Dividend payments

Illustration

Particulars Amount (₹)
Issue of Shares +2,00,000
Loan Repaid -50,000
Dividend Paid -20,000
Net Financing Cash Flow +1,30,000

Example 1 (Business): A company raises funds through a public issue of shares.

Example 2 (Practical): A startup secures a bank loan for expansion.

Topic 7: Interpretation of Cash Flow Statement

Explanation

Interpretation helps understand a company's financial strength and cash management efficiency.

Positive Indicators

  • Positive operating cash flow
  • Adequate liquidity
  • Ability to repay debts
  • Capacity for expansion

Warning Signals

  • Negative operating cash flow
  • Excessive borrowing
  • Frequent cash shortages

Typical Interpretation

Situation Meaning
Positive Operating Cash Flow Healthy operations
Negative Investing Cash Flow Expansion activities
Positive Financing Cash Flow New funding raised
Negative Financing Cash Flow Debt repayment

Example 1 (Business): A company generating positive operating cash flow for several years is generally considered financially stable.

Example 2 (Practical): A small business relying continuously on loans may face future repayment difficulties.

Part B: Inventory Valuation

Topic 8: Inventory Valuation and Income Measurement

Explanation

Inventory refers to goods held for sale or used in production. Inventory valuation affects:

  • Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
  • Gross Profit
  • Net Profit
  • Closing Stock Value

Importance

  • Accurate profit measurement
  • Proper financial reporting
  • Effective inventory management

Example 1 (Business): A supermarket values unsold goods at year-end to determine closing inventory.

Example 2 (Practical): A stationery shop counts remaining stock before calculating annual profit.

Topic 9: Effect of Inventory Valuation Errors on Reported Earnings

Explanation

Errors in inventory valuation directly affect profits.

Understatement of Closing Inventory

Effect Result
COGS Increases
Profit Decreases

Overstatement of Closing Inventory

Effect Result
COGS Decreases
Profit Increases

Example 1 (Business): A company accidentally omits inventory worth ₹1 lakh, causing profit to be understated.

Example 2 (Practical): A retailer counts damaged goods incorrectly, affecting inventory valuation.

Topic 10: Inventory Valuation under Perpetual Inventory System

Explanation

Under the Perpetual Inventory System, inventory records are updated continuously after each transaction.

Advantages

  • Real-time stock information
  • Better inventory control
  • Reduced stock shortages
  • Improved decision-making

Example 1 (Business): Modern retail chains use barcode systems for perpetual inventory tracking.

Example 2 (Practical): An online store updates inventory automatically after each sale.

Topic 11: FIFO Method (First In, First Out)

Explanation

FIFO assumes that goods purchased first are sold first.

Characteristics

  • Oldest inventory issued first.
  • Closing stock consists of latest purchases.

Advantages

  • Simple to understand.
  • Inventory reflects recent costs.
  • Suitable for perishable goods.

Example

Purchase:
100 Units @ ₹10
100 Units @ ₹12

Sold: 150 Units

FIFO Cost:

$$100 \times \text{₹}10 = \text{₹}1,000$$

$$50 \times \text{₹}12 = \text{₹}600$$

$$\text{COGS} = \text{₹}1,600$$

$$\text{Closing Stock} = 50 \times \text{₹}12 = \text{₹}600$$

Example 1 (Business): Reliance Retail may use FIFO for products with expiry dates.

Example 2 (Practical): A dairy shop sells older milk packets before newly arrived stock.

Topic 12: LIFO Method (Last In, First Out)

Explanation

LIFO assumes latest inventory purchased is sold first.

Characteristics

  • Newest inventory issued first.
  • Oldest inventory remains in stock.

Advantages

  • Matches current costs with revenues.
  • Useful during inflation.

Limitations

  • Closing inventory may be outdated.
  • Not permitted under Ind AS/IFRS for external reporting.

Example

Purchase:
100 Units @ ₹10
100 Units @ ₹12

Sold: 150 Units

LIFO Cost:

$$100 \times \text{₹}12 = \text{₹}1,200$$

$$50 \times \text{₹}10 = \text{₹}500$$

$$\text{COGS} = \text{₹}1,700$$

$$\text{Closing Stock} = 50 \times \text{₹}10 = \text{₹}500$$

Example 1 (Business): Some firms use LIFO internally for managerial analysis during inflationary periods.

Example 2 (Practical): A warehouse hypothetically dispatching newly arrived stock first follows LIFO.

Topic 13: Weighted Average Cost Method

Explanation

Under this method, inventory is valued using average cost.

Formula

Average Cost per Unit

$$\text{Weighted Average Cost} = \frac{\text{Total Cost of Inventory}}{\text{Total Units Available}}$$

Illustration

100 Units @ ₹10 = ₹1,000
100 Units @ ₹12 = ₹1,200

$$\text{Total Cost} = \text{₹}2,200$$

$$\text{Total Units} = 200$$

$$\text{Average Cost} = \text{₹}11 \text{ per unit}$$

Advantages

  • Simple and logical.
  • Reduces price fluctuation effects.
  • Widely accepted.

Example 1 (Business): Manufacturing firms often use weighted average cost for raw material valuation.

Example 2 (Practical): A hardware store averages costs when identical items are purchased at different prices.

Comparison of FIFO, LIFO, and Weighted Average

Basis FIFO LIFO Weighted Average
Goods Issued Oldest First Latest First Average Cost
Closing Stock Value Highest (during inflation) Lowest Moderate
Profit During Inflation Higher Lower Moderate
Complexity Easy Moderate Easy
Ind AS/IFRS Acceptance Allowed Not Allowed Allowed
