Unit 3: Financial Statement Analysis

Introduction

Financial Statements provide valuable information about a company's financial performance and position. However, merely preparing financial statements is not enough. Managers, investors, creditors, and other stakeholders need to analyze and interpret the information contained in these statements to make effective decisions.

Financial Statement Analysis involves evaluating a company's financial data using various techniques such as ratio analysis, trend analysis, and inter-firm comparison. It helps assess profitability, liquidity, efficiency, and solvency of a business.

Topic 1: Introduction to Financial Statement Analysis

Explanation

Financial Statement Analysis is the process of examining financial statements to evaluate a company's financial performance and financial health.

It helps answer questions such as:

  • Is the company profitable?
  • Can the company pay its debts?
  • Is the company using its resources efficiently?
  • Is the company's financial position improving?

Objectives of Financial Statement Analysis

  • Evaluate profitability.
  • Measure liquidity and solvency.
  • Assess operational efficiency.
  • Support investment decisions.
  • Facilitate financial planning.

Sources of Financial Analysis

  • Balance Sheet
  • Profit & Loss Account
  • Cash Flow Statement
  • Annual Reports

Example 1 (Business): Infosys analyzes its financial statements regularly to evaluate profitability and operational performance.

Example 2 (Practical): Before lending money, a bank studies the borrower's financial statements to assess repayment capacity.

Topic 3: Nature of Ratio Analysis

Explanation

Ratio Analysis is one of the most widely used tools of financial statement analysis. It expresses the relationship between two financial figures.

Definition

A ratio is the mathematical relationship between two accounting numbers.

Objectives

  • Measure performance.
  • Compare results over time.
  • Compare companies within the industry.
  • Identify strengths and weaknesses.

Types of Ratios

  • Liquidity Ratios
  • Leverage Ratios
  • Activity Ratios
  • Profitability Ratios

Advantages

  • Easy interpretation.
  • Facilitates comparison.
  • Supports decision-making.
  • Identifies trends.

Limitations

  • Based on historical data.
  • Different accounting policies affect comparability.
  • Inflation may distort results.

Example 1 (Business): A company compares current assets and current liabilities to evaluate liquidity.

Example 2 (Practical): An individual compares monthly income and expenses to assess financial stability.

Topic 4: Liquidity Ratios

Explanation

Liquidity Ratios measure a company's ability to meet short-term obligations.

A. Current Ratio

Measures ability to pay current liabilities using current assets.

Formula

$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Ideal Ratio

2 : 1

Interpretation

Ratio Meaning
Above 2 Strong liquidity
Around 2 Satisfactory
Below 1 Liquidity problem

B. Quick Ratio (Acid Test Ratio)

Measures immediate liquidity.

Formula

$$\text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Ideal Ratio

1 : 1

Example 1 (Business): A company with current assets of ₹20 lakh and current liabilities of ₹10 lakh has a current ratio of 2:1.

Example 2 (Practical): A shop owner checks whether available cash and receivables can cover upcoming payments.

Topic 5: Leverage Ratios

Explanation

Leverage Ratios measure the extent to which a business uses borrowed funds. They indicate long-term solvency and financial risk.

Debt-Equity Ratio

Measures relationship between debt and owner's funds.

Formula

$$\text{Debt Equity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}$$

Interpretation

Ratio Meaning
Low Lower financial risk
High Higher financial risk

Proprietary Ratio

Measures proportion of assets financed by owners.

Formula

$$\text{Proprietary Ratio} = \frac{\text{Shareholders' Funds}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

Example 1 (Business): A manufacturing company uses debt-equity ratio to determine whether borrowing levels are appropriate.

Example 2 (Practical): A business owner compares personal investment with bank loans used in the business.

Topic 6: Activity Ratios

Explanation

Activity Ratios measure how efficiently a business utilizes its assets. They are also known as Efficiency Ratios or Turnover Ratios.

Inventory Turnover Ratio

Measures how quickly inventory is sold.

Formula

$$\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}}{\text{Average Inventory}}$$

Interpretation

Higher ratio indicates efficient inventory management.

Debtors Turnover Ratio

Measures collection efficiency.

Formula

$$\text{Debtors Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Credit Sales}}{\text{Average Debtors}}$$

Total Asset Turnover Ratio

Measures efficiency of asset utilization.

Formula

$$\text{Asset Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

Example 1 (Business): A supermarket evaluates inventory turnover to avoid excess stock.

Example 2 (Practical): A bookstore tracks how quickly books are sold to maintain optimal inventory levels.

Topic 7: Profitability Ratios

Explanation

Profitability Ratios measure the ability of a business to generate profits.

Gross Profit Ratio

Measures profitability after deducting direct costs.

Formula

$$\text{Gross Profit Ratio} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}} \times 100$$

Net Profit Ratio

Measures overall profitability.

Formula

$$\text{Net Profit Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}} \times 100$$

Return on Investment (ROI)

Measures return generated on invested funds.

Formula

$$\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Investment}} \times 100$$

Example 1 (Business): Maruti Suzuki evaluates profit margins to improve cost efficiency.

Example 2 (Practical): An entrepreneur compares annual profit with invested capital to calculate ROI.

Explanation

Trend Analysis evaluates financial performance over several years. It identifies patterns of growth or decline.

Advantages

Example 1 (Business): A company studies five years of sales data to evaluate growth trends.

Example 2 (Practical): A family compares yearly household expenses to identify spending patterns.

Topic 9: Inter-Firm Analysis

Explanation

Inter-Firm Analysis compares the performance of one company with another company in the same industry.

Objectives

  • Benchmark performance.
  • Identify competitive position.
  • Improve operational efficiency.

Areas of Comparison

Area Measure
Liquidity Current Ratio
Profitability Net Profit Ratio
Efficiency Asset Turnover
Solvency Debt-Equity Ratio

Advantages

  • Helps identify strengths.
  • Facilitates industry benchmarking.
  • Supports strategic improvements.

Example 1 (Business): Reliance Industries compares profitability ratios with competitors to evaluate performance.

Example 2 (Practical): A local restaurant compares sales growth with nearby restaurants.

Topic 10: Utility and Limitations of Ratio Analysis

Utility of Ratio Analysis

Benefits

  • Measures financial health.
  • Assists management decisions.
  • Helps investors and creditors.
  • Facilitates trend analysis.
  • Enables inter-firm comparison.

Limitations of Ratio Analysis

Limitation Explanation
Historical Data Past performance may not predict future
Inflation Impact Distorts comparisons
Different Accounting Policies Reduces comparability
Window Dressing Financial statements may be manipulated
Ignores Qualitative Factors Cannot measure reputation or employee morale

Example 1 (Business): Two companies may show similar profits but differ significantly in product quality and customer satisfaction.

Example 2 (Practical): A person with high income may still face financial problems due to poor spending habits, which ratios alone may not reveal.
