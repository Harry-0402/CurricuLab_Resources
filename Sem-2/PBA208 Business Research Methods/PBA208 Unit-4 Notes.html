Unit IV: Data Analysis

Introduction

After data has been collected, edited, coded, and tabulated, the next step is Data Analysis. Data analysis is the process of examining, organizing, and interpreting data to discover useful information, draw conclusions, and support decision-making.

In Business Research Methodology, data analysis helps researchers understand relationships among variables, test hypotheses, identify trends, and make recommendations. This unit covers Univariate Analysis, Bivariate Analysis, Multivariate Analysis, and Hypothesis Testing.

Topic 1: Univariate Analysis

Explanation

Univariate Analysis refers to the analysis of a single variable at a time.

The main purpose is to understand the characteristics and distribution of one variable.

Objectives

  • Describe data
  • Identify patterns
  • Measure central tendency
  • Measure dispersion

Measures Used in Univariate Analysis

A. Mean (Average)

Represents the central value of data.

x̄ = Σx / n

Where:

  • x = Observations
  • n = Number of observations

B. Median

Middle value in an ordered dataset.

Example

Data: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50

Median = 30

C. Mode

Most frequently occurring value.

Example

Data: 2, 3, 3, 5, 7

Mode = 3

D. Range

Difference between highest and lowest values.

Range = Maximum − Minimum

Visualization Methods

  • Bar Charts
  • Pie Charts
  • Histograms
  • Frequency Tables

Example 1

A company analyzes employee salaries to determine average compensation.

Example 2

A college studies students' age distribution.

Topic 2: Bivariate Analysis

Explanation

Bivariate Analysis examines the relationship between two variables.

It helps determine whether variables are related and how strongly they are connected.

Objectives

  • Identify relationships
  • Measure associations
  • Test hypotheses

Common Techniques

1. Correlation Analysis

Measures the degree of relationship between variables.

Example

Sales and advertising expenditure.

2. Cross Tabulation

Presents relationships in tabular form.

Example

Gender versus product preference.

3. Scatter Diagram

Graphically shows relationships.

Example

Study hours versus examination marks.

Types of Correlation

Type Meaning
Positive Correlation Both variables move in same direction
Negative Correlation Variables move in opposite directions
Zero Correlation No relationship

Correlation Coefficient

r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / √[Σ(x − x̄)² Σ(y − ȳ)²]

Value of r lies between -1 and +1.

Example 1

A retailer studies the relationship between discounts and sales.

Example 2

A researcher examines attendance and academic performance.

Topic 3: Multivariate Analysis

Explanation

Multivariate Analysis involves the simultaneous analysis of three or more variables.

Business situations often involve multiple factors affecting outcomes.

Objectives

  • Study complex relationships
  • Improve prediction accuracy
  • Support strategic decisions

Applications

Marketing

Customer behavior analysis.

Finance

Investment risk assessment.

Human Resources

Employee performance evaluation.

Healthcare

Patient outcome prediction.

Common Multivariate Techniques

1. Multiple Regression

Analyzes the effect of multiple variables on one dependent variable.

Example

Sales influenced by:

  • Advertising
  • Price
  • Product Quality

2. Factor Analysis

Identifies underlying factors influencing variables.

Example

Customer satisfaction dimensions.

3. Cluster Analysis

Groups similar observations.

Example

Customer segmentation.

4. Discriminant Analysis

Classifies observations into categories.

Example

Credit-risk classification.

Example 1

A bank studies income, age, and credit score to predict loan repayment.

Example 2

A university analyzes attendance, assignments, and study hours to predict academic performance.

Topic 4: Hypothesis Testing

Explanation

Hypothesis Testing is a statistical method used to determine whether there is enough evidence to support a research hypothesis.

It helps researchers make decisions based on sample data.

Concept of Hypothesis Testing

Researchers begin with:

Null Hypothesis (H₀)

Assumes no relationship or difference exists.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)

Assumes a relationship or difference exists.

Example

H₀: Training does not improve productivity.

H₁: Training improves productivity.

Hypothesis Testing Process

Formulate Hypotheses
↓
Select Significance Level
↓
Collect Sample Data
↓
Calculate Test Statistic
↓
Compare Results
↓
Accept or Reject H₀
↓
Draw Conclusion

Types of Errors in Hypothesis Testing

Type I Error (α Error)

Rejecting a true null hypothesis.

Example

Concluding a new product is successful when it is not.

Type II Error (β Error)

Accepting a false null hypothesis.

Example

Concluding a new product is unsuccessful when it is actually successful.

Comparison of Errors

Error Type Meaning
Type I Error Reject true H₀
Type II Error Accept false H₀

Significance Level

The significance level represents the probability of making a Type I Error.

Common values:

  • 0.05 (5%)
  • 0.01 (1%)

Steps in Hypothesis Testing

Step 1

State Null and Alternative Hypotheses.

Step 2

Choose Significance Level.

Step 3

Select Statistical Test.

Step 4

Calculate Test Statistic.

Step 5

Determine Critical Value or p-value.

Step 6

Make Decision.

Step 7

Interpret Results.

Example of Hypothesis Testing

A company wants to determine whether a new training program improves employee productivity.

H₀

Training has no effect on productivity.

H₁

Training improves productivity.

Data is collected and analyzed using statistical tests.

The results determine whether H₀ should be accepted or rejected.

  • Reduce uncertainty

Quick Revision Summary

  • Data analysis converts raw data into useful information.
  • Univariate analysis studies one variable.
  • Bivariate analysis studies relationships between two variables.
  • Multivariate analysis studies multiple variables simultaneously.
  • Hypothesis testing evaluates assumptions using statistical methods.
  • Type I and Type II errors are important concepts in hypothesis testing.
  • Data analysis supports evidence-based decision-making.
