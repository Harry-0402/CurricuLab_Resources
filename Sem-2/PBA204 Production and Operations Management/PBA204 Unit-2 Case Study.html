Case Study: Improving Productivity Through Work Study at Amazon Fulfillment Centers

1. Company Background

Amazon is one of the world's largest e-commerce and logistics organizations, serving millions of customers through an extensive network of fulfillment centers. The company operates a highly integrated supply chain that includes warehousing, inventory management, packaging, and last-mile delivery services. Amazon's business model relies heavily on operational efficiency, rapid order fulfillment, and customer satisfaction. In recent years, the company has experienced significant growth in online retail demand, leading to increased pressure on fulfillment operations. While revenue and order volumes continue to grow, management faces ongoing challenges in maintaining productivity levels and controlling operational costs across its warehouse network.

2. Business Context

The e-commerce industry is characterized by intense competition, shrinking delivery windows, and rising customer expectations. Customers increasingly expect same-day or next-day delivery, requiring fulfillment centers to process larger volumes of orders with greater speed and accuracy.

At the same time, labor costs have increased due to inflation and workforce shortages. Competitors have invested heavily in warehouse automation and process optimization to improve efficiency. Amazon's management believes that productivity improvements can be achieved not only through automation but also through systematic work study techniques, process mapping, and performance measurement.

A recent internal review identified significant productivity differences among fulfillment centers despite similar technology and staffing levels. Senior management has therefore launched a productivity improvement initiative focused on work study and process optimization.

3. Strategic Problem Situation

One of Amazon's largest fulfillment centers in Texas has experienced declining operational efficiency over the past two years. Although order volume has increased substantially, productivity growth has not kept pace. Management has observed longer order processing times, increased overtime expenses, and declining employee utilization rates.

Operations managers conducted preliminary investigations and found substantial variation in work methods among employees performing identical tasks. Different picking routes, inconsistent packaging procedures, and varying workstation layouts have contributed to inefficiencies.

Employees argue that increasing workload and product variety have made tasks more complex. Supervisors believe performance standards are outdated and fail to reflect current operational realities. Finance managers are concerned about rising labor costs, while customer service teams report increasing complaints related to delayed shipments.

Management is considering a comprehensive work study program involving method study, process mapping, work measurement, standard time development, and work sampling. However, implementing these initiatives would require temporary disruption to operations, additional training, and investment in industrial engineering resources.

The leadership team must determine how productivity can be improved without negatively affecting employee morale or customer service performance.

5. Decision Dilemma

Amazon's operations leadership must decide how aggressively to pursue work study initiatives.

Option 1: Focus on employee training and standardized work methods.

Option 2: Redesign warehouse processes using detailed process mapping and work measurement findings.

Option 3: Combine method study, work measurement, and selective automation investments.

Each alternative offers different benefits and risks. Training is less expensive but may deliver limited gains. Process redesign can generate substantial efficiency improvements but may disrupt operations. Combining redesign with automation could maximize productivity gains but requires higher investment and implementation complexity.

Management must choose the most appropriate productivity improvement strategy.

Question 2

Model Answers

Answer 1

The data indicate that declining productivity is primarily driven by inefficient work methods and excessive non-value-added activities. Employee productivity fell from 52 orders per hour in 2023 to 44 orders per hour in 2025, while average processing time increased from 16.2 to 19.5 minutes per order.

Process mapping findings reveal significant waste. Employees lose 2.4 minutes per order walking between aisles, 1.1 minutes waiting for inventory updates, 0.8 minutes due to packaging rework, and 0.7 minutes from equipment downtime. Together, these non-value-added activities account for approximately five minutes per order, explaining much of the productivity decline.

Work sampling further supports this conclusion. Only 68% of employee time is spent on productive activities, while 20% is consumed by waiting and material handling delays. This suggests process inefficiencies rather than workforce capability issues.

Using work measurement data, the observed time is 16.5 minutes. Applying the performance rating of 110% gives a normal time of 18.15 minutes. Including a 15% allowance results in a standard time of approximately 20.87 minutes. This indicates that current operational standards may require revision and process redesign.

Management should prioritize reducing aisle travel, improving inventory system responsiveness, minimizing packaging errors, and enhancing equipment reliability. These actions address the largest productivity losses while improving service performance and reducing labor costs.

Answer 2

Option 3, combining method study, work measurement, and selective automation, provides the strongest strategic solution.

Option 1 focuses on training and standardization. While this may improve consistency, it does not adequately address structural inefficiencies such as excessive walking distances, inventory delays, and equipment downtime. Therefore, benefits are likely to be limited.

Option 2 addresses process inefficiencies through redesign and work study techniques. This approach directly targets waste identified in process mapping and work sampling results. However, without technological support, future scalability may remain constrained as order volumes continue growing.

Option 3 integrates operational improvement with targeted automation. This approach enables Amazon to standardize best practices, redesign workflows, establish accurate performance standards, and automate repetitive activities where economically justified.

The business case is compelling. Labor costs increased from $78 million to $108 million within three years, while overtime costs more than doubled. Management forecasts annual savings of approximately $12 million through productivity improvements. Even moderate productivity gains of 15–20% would significantly improve operational performance and customer service levels.

Selective automation should focus on inventory tracking, picking assistance, and packaging support while preserving workforce flexibility. Combined with method study and standard time development, this strategy creates sustainable productivity improvements while maintaining adaptability for future growth.

Therefore, Option 3 offers the most balanced combination of efficiency improvement, long-term scalability, and operational sustainability.
