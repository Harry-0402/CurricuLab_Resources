Unit 3 – Company Law (Companies Act, 1956)

1. Meaning of Company and Forms of Company

Introduction

A company is one of the most important forms of business organization in modern commerce. Unlike sole proprietorships or partnerships, a company is recognized as a separate legal entity created under law.

The Companies Act, 1956 governs the formation, regulation, management, and dissolution of companies in India. A company can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued in its own name.

The development of companies allowed large businesses to raise capital from many investors and operate with limited liability.

Key Concepts

  • Company

  • Separate Legal Entity

  • Artificial Person

  • Limited Liability

  • Perpetual Succession

  • Common Seal

  • Share Capital

Detailed Explanation

Definition of Company

A company may be defined as:

“An association of persons formed for carrying on business with a view to earning profit and registered under the Companies Act.”

A company comes into existence only after registration under the Companies Act.

Characteristics of a Company

FeatureExplanation
Separate Legal EntityThe company is legally separate from its shareholders.
Limited LiabilityShareholders are liable only up to the amount invested.
Perpetual SuccessionThe company continues even if members change.
Transferability of SharesShares can be transferred easily.
Artificial PersonIt exists only in the eyes of law.
Common SealActs as the official signature of the company.

Important Case Example

Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd. (1897)

  • Mr. Salomon formed a company with his family members.

  • The company later became insolvent.

  • Creditors tried to make Mr. Salomon personally liable.

Judgment:
The court held that the company is a separate legal entity from its members, so Mr. Salomon was not personally liable.

This case established the principle of separate corporate personality.

Forms (Types) of Companies

Companies can be classified in several ways.

1. On the Basis of Liability

1. Company Limited by Shares

Members’ liability is limited to the amount unpaid on their shares.

Example

If a person owns shares worth ₹10,000 and has already paid ₹7,000, the maximum liability is ₹3,000.

2. Company Limited by Guarantee

Members agree to contribute a fixed amount in case of company liquidation.

Usually used for:

  • Non-profit organizations

  • Clubs

  • Societies

3. Unlimited Company

Members have unlimited liability similar to partnerships.

However, this type of company is rare in practice.

2. On the Basis of Membership

1. Private Company

A private company is defined as a company which:

  • Restricts transfer of shares

  • Limits number of members

  • Prohibits invitation to the public for shares

Characteristics

FeatureLimit
Minimum members2
Maximum members50 (under 1956 Act)
Minimum directors2

Example

Family-owned businesses.

2. Public Company

A public company is one that:

  • Allows free transfer of shares

  • Can invite public to subscribe shares

  • Has larger membership

Characteristics

FeatureRequirement
Minimum members7
Maximum membersNo limit
Minimum directors3

Public companies raise capital from the stock market.

3. On the Basis of Control

Holding Company

A company that controls another company by owning more than 50% shares.

Subsidiary Company

A company controlled by another company.

Example structure:

Holding Company
       ↓
Subsidiary Company
       ↓
Sub-Subsidiary Company

4. Government Company

A company in which 51% or more share capital is owned by the Government.

Examples:

  • Public sector enterprises

  • Government corporations

Real-World Example

Reliance Industries Ltd.

  • A public company

  • Shares traded on stock exchange

  • Thousands of shareholders

  • Managed by board of directors

This demonstrates how companies allow large-scale capital formation and business expansion.

Summary of this Section

  • A company is an artificial legal person created under the Companies Act.

  • It has features like separate legal identity, limited liability, and perpetual succession.

  • Companies can be classified based on:

    • Liability

    • Membership

    • Control

  • The two major types are private company and public company.

2. Formation of a Company

Introduction

The formation of a company refers to the legal process through which a company is created and recognized as a separate legal entity under the Companies Act. A company does not exist naturally; it comes into existence only after completing certain legal formalities and registration procedures.

The process generally involves promotion, registration, and commencement of business.

Key Concepts

  • Promotion

  • Promoters

  • Registration

  • Incorporation

  • Certificate of Incorporation

  • Certificate of Commencement of Business

Stages in the Formation of a Company

Idea of Business
      ↓
Promotion Stage
      ↓
Registration / Incorporation
      ↓
Certificate of Incorporation
      ↓
Certificate of Commencement of Business
      ↓
Company Starts Business

1. Promotion Stage

Promotion is the first stage in the formation of a company. It involves discovering a business opportunity and taking necessary steps to establish a company.

Promoter

A promoter is a person who undertakes to form a company and takes necessary steps to bring it into existence.

Promoters may include:

  • Entrepreneurs

  • Professionals

  • Financial institutions

Functions of Promoters

FunctionExplanation
Idea generationIdentify a profitable business idea
Feasibility studyAnalyze financial and technical viability
Assembling resourcesArrange capital, land, and personnel
Preparation of documentsDraft Memorandum and Articles of Association
Appointment of professionalsHire lawyers, accountants, and advisors

Duties of Promoters

Promoters have fiduciary duties towards the company.

Important duties include:

  1. Duty of good faith

  2. Duty to disclose profits

  3. Duty not to make secret profits

  4. Duty to avoid conflict of interest

If promoters make secret profits, the company may:

  • Recover the profit

  • Cancel the contract

3. Memorandum of Association (MOA)

Introduction

The Memorandum of Association is the most important document of a company. It defines the constitution and scope of activities of the company.

It acts as the charter of the company and determines the relationship between the company and the outside world.

Purpose of MOA

  • Defines company objectives

  • Specifies powers of company

  • Limits company activities

A company cannot operate beyond the scope defined in the memorandum.

This rule is called the Doctrine of Ultra Vires.

Contents of Memorandum of Association

ClauseMeaning
Name ClauseName of the company
Registered Office ClauseLocation of company’s registered office
Object ClauseMain objectives of the company
Liability ClauseLiability of members
Capital ClauseShare capital of the company
Subscription ClauseNames of subscribers to memorandum

Example

A company formed to manufacture automobiles cannot legally start banking business, because it falls outside its object clause.

4. Articles of Association (AOA)

Introduction

The Articles of Association contain the rules and regulations for internal management of the company.

While the MOA defines the company’s powers, the AOA explains how those powers are exercised.

Purpose of AOA

  • Regulate internal administration

  • Define rights and duties of directors

  • Specify procedures for meetings

  • Provide rules for share transfer

Common Contents of AOA

ProvisionExplanation
Share transfer rulesConditions for transferring shares
Share capital rulesIssue and allotment of shares
Directors’ powersAppointment and removal of directors
MeetingsProcedures for general meetings
Voting rightsRules for voting

Difference Between MOA and AOA

BasisMemorandum of AssociationArticles of Association
NatureCharter of companyInternal rules
ScopeDefines company powersDefines company management
ImportanceSuperior documentSubordinate document
AlterationDifficult to alterEasier to alter

5. Prospectus

Introduction

A prospectus is a document issued by a public company inviting the public to subscribe to shares or debentures.

It contains detailed information about the company so that investors can make informed decisions.

Objectives of Prospectus

  • Inform public about investment opportunity

  • Provide financial information

  • Encourage investors to buy shares

Contents of Prospectus

InformationDetails
Company detailsName, address, objectives
Capital structureAuthorized and issued capital
Financial informationProfits, assets, liabilities
DirectorsNames and qualifications
Risk factorsPotential risks for investors

Liability for Misstatement

If the prospectus contains false or misleading information, the following persons may be liable:

  • Directors

  • Promoters

  • Experts

Liability may be:

  • Civil liability

  • Criminal liability

Example

If a company falsely claims high profits to attract investors and the investors suffer losses, they can sue the company for misrepresentation.

Summary of This Part

  • Company formation involves promotion, incorporation, and commencement of business.

  • Promoters are responsible for bringing the company into existence.

  • The Memorandum of Association (MOA) defines the company’s scope and powers.

  • The Articles of Association (AOA) govern internal management.

  • A prospectus invites the public to invest in company shares.

6. Share Allotment

Introduction

Share allotment refers to the process of distributing shares to applicants who have applied for them. When a company issues shares, investors submit applications. The company then decides how many shares each applicant will receive.

This process creates a legal relationship between the company and the shareholder.

Key Concepts

  • Share application

  • Share allotment

  • Minimum subscription

  • Allotment letter

  • Return of allotment

Conditions for Valid Allotment

ConditionExplanation
Minimum subscriptionRequired amount must be received before allotment
Application moneyApplicants must pay the prescribed amount
Board approvalDirectors must approve allotment
Allotment within reasonable timeShares must be allotted promptly

Example

If a company issues 10,000 shares, and receives applications for 15,000 shares, it may allot shares proportionally.

7. Shares and Share Capital

Introduction

A share represents a unit of ownership in a company. A person holding shares is called a shareholder or member.

Share capital refers to the total amount of money raised by issuing shares.

Types of Share Capital

TypeMeaning

Issued CapitalCapital offered to investors
Subscribed CapitalCapital actually subscribed by investors
Paid-up CapitalAmount actually paid by shareholders

Types of Shares

TypeDescription
Equity SharesCarry voting rights
Preference SharesPriority in dividend and capital repayment

8. Promoters

Introduction

Promoters are the individuals responsible for initiating the process of forming a company.

They perform tasks such as:

  • Identifying business opportunities

  • Preparing documents

  • Arranging capital

  • Registering the company

Legal Position of Promoters

Promoters are not agents of the company, because the company does not exist yet.

However, they have a fiduciary relationship with the company.

Remedies Against Promoters

If promoters misuse their position:

  • Company may recover secret profits

  • Company may rescind contracts

  • Promoters may be liable for damages

9. Membership of a Company

Introduction

Members are the owners of the company.

A person becomes a member by:

  • Subscribing to the Memorandum

  • Purchasing shares

  • Transfer of shares

  • Transmission of shares

Rights of Members

RightExplanation
Voting rightsParticipate in company decisions
Dividend rightsShare in company profits
Right to inspect recordsAccess company documents
Right to attend meetingsParticipate in general meetings

10. Meetings and Proceedings

Introduction

Meetings are essential for collective decision-making in companies.

Important company decisions are made through formal meetings.

Types of Meetings

MeetingPurpose
Statutory MeetingFirst meeting of public company
Annual General Meeting (AGM)Annual review of company activities
Extraordinary General Meeting (EGM)Called for urgent matters
Board MeetingMeeting of directors

Quorum

Quorum means the minimum number of members required to conduct a meeting.

Without quorum, the meeting cannot proceed legally.

11. Directors

Introduction

Directors are the individuals responsible for managing the company’s affairs.

They form the Board of Directors, which is the governing body of the company.

Functions of Directors

FunctionExplanation
Policy makingDetermine company strategy
Financial controlManage company funds
AppointmentHire managers and executives
RepresentationAct on behalf of company

Duties of Directors

  • Duty of good faith

  • Duty of care and diligence

  • Duty to avoid conflict of interest

  • Duty to act within powers

12. Managers and Secretary

Manager

A manager is responsible for day-to-day administration of the company.

Company Secretary

The company secretary ensures that the company complies with legal and regulatory requirements.

13. Prevention of Oppression and Mismanagement

Introduction

Sometimes majority shareholders may misuse their power against minority shareholders.

To prevent this, the law provides remedies for:

  • Oppression

  • Mismanagement

Oppression

Oppression occurs when majority shareholders act unfairly toward minority shareholders.

Example:

  • Denying voting rights

  • Misuse of company funds

Mismanagement

Mismanagement occurs when the company’s affairs are conducted in a harmful or negligent manner.

Example:

  • Financial irregularities

  • Poor management decisions

Remedy

Affected members can apply to the Company Law Board (now National Company Law Tribunal – NCLT) for relief.

14. Winding Up of a Company

Introduction

Winding up refers to the process of closing a company and distributing its assets among creditors and shareholders.

It is also called liquidation.

Modes of Winding Up

TypeMeaning
Compulsory winding upOrdered by court

Example

If a company suffers continuous losses and cannot pay debts, it may be wound up through court order or voluntary decision.
