Unit 2 – Law of Sales of Goods Act, 1930

1. Introduction to the Sale of Goods Act, 1930

Introduction

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 governs the buying and selling of goods in India. It defines the rights and duties of buyers and sellers and regulates commercial transactions involving movable goods.

The Act was originally a part of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, but later it was separated to specifically regulate contracts related to the sale of goods.

The Act applies to the whole of India and mainly deals with contracts where ownership of goods is transferred from seller to buyer for a price.

Key Concepts

  • Contract of Sale

  • Buyer

  • Seller

  • Goods

  • Price

  • Transfer of ownership

Detailed Explanation

A contract of sale of goods is a contract where the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the ownership of goods to the buyer in exchange for money.

The essential elements are:

  1. Two Parties

    • Seller

    • Buyer

  2. Goods

    • Goods must be movable property.

  3. Transfer of Ownership

    • Ownership must pass from seller to buyer.

  4. Price

    • Payment must be in money.

Legal Definition

Under Section 4 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930:

A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price.

Types of Contract of Sale

A contract of sale can be of two types:

TypeMeaning
SaleOwnership of goods is transferred immediately
Agreement to SellOwnership is transferred at a future time

Flow of Contract of Sale

Seller offers goods
        ↓
Buyer agrees to purchase
        ↓
Price is fixed
        ↓
Ownership transferred
        ↓
Buyer receives goods

Example

Suppose Rahul sells his laptop to Aman for ₹40,000.

  • Rahul → Seller

  • Aman → Buyer

  • Laptop → Goods

  • ₹40,000 → Price

Ownership of the laptop transfers from Rahul to Aman.

Summary of this Section

  • The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 regulates contracts related to sale and purchase of goods.

  • A contract of sale involves transfer of ownership of goods for money.

  • It includes two types: Sale and Agreement to Sell.

  • The Act ensures legal protection to both buyers and sellers.

2. General Principles of Sale of Goods

Introduction

The Sale of Goods Act establishes several basic principles governing transactions involving goods. These principles ensure fairness, clarity, and legal protection in commercial dealings.

Key Concepts

  • Contract of sale

  • Transfer of property

  • Goods classification

  • Rights of buyer and seller

  • Delivery of goods

Detailed Explanation

Some important general principles of the Sale of Goods Act include:

1. Contract of Sale

A valid sale of goods must satisfy all essential elements of a valid contract, such as:

  • Offer and acceptance

  • Consideration

  • Free consent

  • Legal object

  • Capacity of parties

2. Goods Must Be Movable

The Act applies only to movable goods.

Examples:

  • Furniture

  • Vehicles

  • Machinery

  • Electronics

It does not apply to immovable property like land or buildings.

Types of Goods

TypeMeaning
Existing GoodsGoods already owned by seller
Future GoodsGoods to be produced later
Contingent GoodsGoods dependent on uncertain event

Explanation of Goods Types

Existing Goods

Goods that are already manufactured and owned by the seller at the time of contract.

Example:
A shop selling already available smartphones.

Future Goods

Goods that will be manufactured or acquired later.

Example:
A factory agreeing to deliver 100 chairs after production.

Contingent Goods

Goods whose sale depends on an uncertain event.

Example:
Selling goods that will be received only if a shipment arrives safely.

Table: Classification of Goods

CategoryDescriptionExample
Existing goodsAlready availableLaptop in store
Future goodsTo be producedFurniture order
Contingent goodsDepends on eventImported goods shipment

Example / Case Study

A company agrees to supply 500 computers to a school after manufacturing them next month.

  • These computers are future goods because they do not exist at the time of contract.

Summary of this Section

  • The Sale of Goods Act applies to movable goods only.

  • A valid sale requires all elements of a valid contract.

  • Goods are classified as existing, future, or contingent goods.

  • The Act regulates transfer of ownership, delivery, and payment in commercial transactions.

3. Conditions and Warranties

Introduction

In a contract of sale, the terms of the contract are divided into conditions and warranties. These terms define the rights and obligations of the buyer and seller. The distinction is important because the legal remedies differ if these terms are breached.

Key Concepts

  • Condition

  • Warranty

  • Breach of condition

  • Breach of warranty

  • Implied conditions

Detailed Explanation

1. Condition

A condition is a fundamental term of the contract. It goes to the root of the contract.

If a condition is breached, the aggrieved party may:

  • Cancel the contract, and

  • Claim damages

Legal Meaning:
A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract.

Example

A person purchases a car stating that it must be brand new.

If the seller delivers a used car, the condition is violated.
The buyer can reject the car and cancel the contract.

2. Warranty

A warranty is a secondary or minor term in the contract.

If a warranty is breached:

  • The buyer cannot cancel the contract

  • The buyer can only claim damages

Example

A person buys a laptop with a promise that the battery lasts 8 hours.

If it lasts only 6 hours, it is a breach of warranty.
The buyer cannot return the laptop but can claim compensation.

Comparison: Condition vs Warranty

BasisConditionWarranty
ImportanceEssential termSecondary term
Effect of breachContract can be terminatedOnly damages can be claimed
ImpactGoes to root of contractMinor impact

When Condition Becomes Warranty

Sometimes a condition may be treated as a warranty.

This happens when:

  1. The buyer chooses to accept the goods despite the defect.

  2. The contract cannot be rejected due to partial performance.

Example

A buyer orders 500 chairs, but 10 chairs are defective.

Instead of rejecting the entire order, the buyer may keep the chairs and claim damages.

Summary of this Section

  • Conditions are essential terms of a contract.

  • Warranties are minor contractual terms.

  • Breach of condition allows contract termination and damages.

  • Breach of warranty allows damages only.

4. Doctrine of Caveat Emptor

Introduction

The doctrine “Caveat Emptor” is a Latin phrase meaning “Let the buyer beware.”

This principle states that the buyer is responsible for checking the quality and suitability of goods before purchasing them.

The seller is generally not responsible for defects unless certain exceptions apply.

Key Concepts

  • Caveat Emptor

  • Buyer responsibility

  • Exceptions to caveat emptor

  • Implied conditions

Detailed Explanation

Under the doctrine of caveat emptor:

The buyer must:

  • Examine the goods carefully

  • Ensure they are suitable for their purpose

If the buyer fails to do so, the seller is not liable for defects.

Example

A person buys a second-hand car without inspection.

Later the buyer finds engine problems.

Under caveat emptor, the buyer cannot claim damages because they did not inspect the car.

Exceptions to Caveat Emptor

The law protects buyers in several situations where the seller cannot hide defects.

1. Fitness for Purpose

If the buyer informs the seller of the purpose, the goods must be suitable for that purpose.

Example:
A buyer asks for paint suitable for outdoor walls, but the seller provides indoor paint.

2. Merchantable Quality

Goods must be of reasonable quality and usable condition.

Example:
Buying a sealed food product that is spoiled.

3. Fraud or Misrepresentation

If the seller hides defects intentionally, the buyer can claim damages.

4. Sale by Description

Goods must match the description given by the seller.

Example:
Selling pure silk fabric that turns out to be synthetic.

Flowchart: Caveat Emptor Principle

Buyer inspects goods
        ↓
If defect visible → Buyer responsible
        ↓
If hidden defect or fraud → Seller liable

Example / Case Study

A customer asks a pharmacist for medicine suitable for allergy treatment.

The pharmacist gives the wrong medicine.

Since the buyer relied on the seller's expertise, the seller is liable for damages.

Summary of this Section

  • Caveat Emptor means “Let the buyer beware.”

  • Buyers must inspect goods before purchase.

  • However, several legal exceptions protect buyers such as:

    • Fitness for purpose

    • Merchantable quality

    • Fraud

    • Sale by description.

5. Transfer of Ownership (Property in Goods)

Introduction

One of the most important aspects of the Sale of Goods Act is the transfer of ownership (property) from seller to buyer.

Ownership determines:

  • Who bears the risk

  • Who has legal rights over the goods

Key Concepts

  • Transfer of property

  • Risk transfer

  • Specific goods

  • Unascertained goods

Detailed Explanation

Rule 1: Transfer of Ownership in Specific Goods

Ownership transfers when the parties intend it to transfer.

Example:
Buying a specific car in a showroom.

Rule 2: Transfer of Ownership in Unascertained Goods

Ownership transfers only when goods are identified and separated from the bulk.

Example:
Buying 10 bags of rice from a warehouse stock of 500 bags.

Ownership transfers only when those 10 bags are separated.

Rule 3: Risk Follows Ownership

Once ownership is transferred:

  • Risk also transfers to the buyer

Example:
If goods are damaged after ownership transfer, the buyer bears the loss.

Table: Ownership Transfer Rules

SituationOwnership Transfer
Specific goodsWhen contract is made
Future goodsWhen goods are produced
Unascertained goodsWhen goods are identified

Example / Case Study

A customer purchases a washing machine and ownership transfers immediately.

Before delivery, the shop catches fire.

Since ownership had already passed to the buyer, the loss is borne by the buyer.

Summary of this Section

  • Ownership determines legal responsibility for goods.

  • Ownership passes according to the intention of the parties.

  • Risk usually follows ownership.

6. Performance of a Contract of Sale

Introduction

The performance of a contract of sale refers to the fulfillment of the obligations agreed upon by the buyer and the seller. According to the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, both parties must perform their duties as stated in the contract.

Performance mainly involves:

  • Delivery of goods by the seller

  • Payment of price by the buyer

Key Concepts

  • Delivery of goods

  • Payment of price

  • Time and place of delivery

  • Types of delivery

Detailed Explanation

Duties of the Seller

The seller must:

  1. Deliver the goods according to the contract.

  2. Ensure the goods match the description and quality.

  3. Deliver goods at the agreed time and place.

Duties of the Buyer

The buyer must:

  1. Accept the goods.

  2. Pay the agreed price.

  3. Take delivery of goods when offered.

Types of Delivery

TypeMeaning
Actual DeliveryPhysical transfer of goods
Constructive DeliveryOwnership transferred without physical delivery
Symbolic DeliveryTransfer through symbols like keys or documents

Explanation

Actual Delivery

Goods are physically handed over to the buyer.

Example:
A shopkeeper gives a customer a mobile phone after payment.

Constructive Delivery

A third party holding the goods acknowledges that they now hold them for the buyer.

Example:
Goods stored in a warehouse are transferred to the buyer through documentation.

Symbolic Delivery

Delivery occurs through a symbol representing goods.

Example:
Handing over keys of a warehouse containing goods.

Flow of Contract Performance

Contract formed
      ↓
Seller delivers goods
      ↓
Buyer accepts goods
      ↓
Buyer pays price

Example

A retailer orders 100 laptops from a supplier.

  • Supplier delivers laptops → Seller performs obligation

  • Retailer pays agreed price → Buyer performs obligation

Thus, the contract is successfully performed.

Summary of this Section

  • Performance involves delivery of goods and payment of price.

  • Both seller and buyer must fulfill their duties.

  • Delivery can be actual, constructive, or symbolic.

7. Remedial Measures (Remedies for Breach)

Introduction

When either the buyer or the seller fails to perform the contract, the law provides remedies to the aggrieved party. These remedies protect the rights of both parties.

Key Concepts

  • Breach of contract

  • Seller’s remedies

  • Buyer’s remedies

  • Damages

Detailed Explanation

Remedies Available to the Seller

If the buyer fails to pay or accept goods, the seller can:

  1. Suit for Price

    • Seller can sue the buyer to recover the price.

  2. Suit for Damages

    • Seller can claim compensation for losses.

  3. Right of Lien

    • Seller can retain possession of goods until payment is made.

  4. Stoppage in Transit

    • Seller can stop goods during transport if the buyer becomes insolvent.

Remedies Available to the Buyer

If the seller fails to deliver goods, the buyer can:

  1. Suit for Damages

    • Claim compensation for losses.

  2. Suit for Specific Performance

    • Court may order the seller to deliver goods.

  3. Suit for Breach of Warranty

    • Claim damages if warranty is violated.

  4. Reject Goods

    • Buyer can reject goods if conditions are not fulfilled.

Table: Remedies in Sale of Goods

PartyRemedy
SellerSuit for price
SellerDamages
SellerLien on goods
SellerStoppage in transit
BuyerDamages
BuyerSpecific performance
BuyerReject goods

Example / Case Study

A buyer orders 50 computers, but the seller refuses to deliver them.

The buyer can:

  • File a suit for damages, or

  • Ask the court for specific performance.

Summary of this Section

  • Remedies protect the rights of both buyer and seller.

  • Sellers can claim price, damages, lien, and stoppage in transit.

  • Buyers can claim damages or specific performance.

Part B – Indian Partnership Act, 1932

8. Meaning and Definition of Partnership

Introduction

The Indian Partnership Act, 1932 governs partnerships in India. A partnership is formed when two or more persons agree to carry on a business and share profits.

Key Concepts

  • Partnership

  • Partners

  • Firm

  • Partnership deed

  • Mutual agency

Legal Definition

According to Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932:

Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.

Elements of Partnership

A partnership must have:

  1. Two or more persons

  2. Agreement between partners

  3. Business activity

  4. Profit sharing

  5. Mutual agency

Explanation of Mutual Agency

Mutual agency means:

  • Every partner is an agent and principal of the firm.

  • One partner's actions can bind the entire firm.

Example

Three people start a clothing business together.

If one partner signs a contract with a supplier, the entire partnership firm becomes legally bound.

Table: Key Partnership Terms

TermMeaning
PartnerMember of partnership
FirmCollective name of partners
Partnership deedWritten agreement between partners

Summary of this Section

  • Partnership is a business relationship between two or more persons.

  • Partners share profits and responsibilities.

  • The most important feature is mutual agency.

9. Formation of Partnership

Introduction

Partnership is formed through an agreement between partners. This agreement can be written or oral.

Partnership Deed

A partnership deed is a document that defines:

  • Profit sharing ratio

  • Duties of partners

  • Capital contribution

  • Rules for dispute resolution

Common Contents of Partnership Deed

ClauseDescription
Firm nameName of partnership
Capital contributionInvestment by partners
Profit sharingPercentage of profit
DutiesResponsibilities of partners
Admission of new partnerRules for new members

Example

Four friends start a restaurant business and sign a partnership deed specifying:

  • Capital contribution

  • Profit sharing ratio

  • Management duties

Summary of this Section

  • Partnership is formed through agreement among partners.

  • A partnership deed defines rights and responsibilities.

10. Rights and Liabilities of Partners

Rights of Partners

  1. Right to participate in business

  2. Right to share profits

  3. Right to access books of accounts

  4. Right to be consulted in decisions

Liabilities of Partners

  1. Liability for firm debts

  2. Duty of good faith

  3. Duty to act in firm’s interest

  4. Duty to compensate for negligence

Example

If one partner misuses company funds, they must compensate the firm for losses.

11. Dissolution of Partnership

Introduction

Dissolution means ending the partnership relationship between partners.

Types of Dissolution

TypeMeaning
Dissolution by agreementPartners mutually agree
Dissolution by noticePartner gives notice
Dissolution by courtCourt orders dissolution

Reasons for Dissolution

  • Expiry of partnership period

  • Death of partner

  • Insolvency of partner

  • Completion of business

Example

Three partners start a construction company for a specific project.

Once the project is completed, the partnership automatically dissolves.

Summary of this Section

  • Dissolution means termination of partnership relations.

  • It may occur by agreement, notice, or court order.
